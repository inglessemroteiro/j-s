How I make sure a customer feels heard

Every new customer starts with a 30-minute call with my team. I wrote the manual for that call. It is built around four moments, in this order.

01

Ask what stopped them

They have wanted this for years. Something got in the way.

02

Stop and name the pain

Where most opportunities are lost. Say it back before moving on.

03

Ask before pricing

“What would make you decide today?” The objection surfaces early.

04

Follow up in their words

Never a generic message. Always the thing they actually told us.

A real example of moment 02 going wrong. A customer told us she wanted to speak to her husband's family, go to church and do everyday things without needing a translator. She was not talking about English. She was talking about not needing anybody in order to exist where she lives. The team asked the next scheduled question instead of stopping there. That conversation is in the manual as a missed opportunity, so nobody repeats it.

The five rules the team follows in every conversation

Following the protocol→Listening to the person
Moving to the next question→Stopping to name what we heard
A generic message→The words she used
Artificial urgency→Real authority
Asking if she checked with her husband→Treating her as the one who decides

How I sell without sounding like a script

Most of our customers arrive through recommendations from existing ones, which only happens when people were sold the right thing. The manual has a dictionary of the eight objections we hear most, what each one really means, and how to answer it. Three examples:

What she means: she is not short of time. She thinks the course needs several hours a week.

How we answer: with a question. How hard was it to find this one hour to speak to me? Because one hour a week is the whole commitment.

What she means: price becomes an objection when the value has not landed.

How we answer: the rule is not to discount before finding out whether it did. First the connection, then the payment options if they are still needed.

What she means: the most emotional objection there is. She is not doubting us, she is protecting herself from being disappointed again.

How we answer: by agreeing that stopping made sense, before anything else.
The reference example in the manual

A customer said she would have to miss three classes and asked whether to wait for the next cycle. The team gave an honest opinion, that waiting would postpone the thing she said she wanted, and offered a real alternative: private lessons now, a group in August. She enrolled. No discount and no pressure.

What happens when a customer wants to cancel

The hardest customer conversation in my business is the one where somebody wants to leave. I wrote a fixed procedure for it after the first time I improvised and lost a customer who could have stayed with a different timetable. Cancelling for us is the last option, we have created ways to make it work for our client.

1Offer a different groupSome clients have to change their work schedule, but this doesn't mean they need to stop their English Classes.
2Offer private lessons every two weeksThis gives them more flexibility and they keep studying even when life gets hard. 
3CancellationOnly after the first two have been offered and turned down. Then a formal termination both sides sign and a thank-you note.

Before any of this: the moment a student's attendance drops, they hear from us. We built a system in which we keep track of students' attendance so they're engaged into the school's activities.

How I keep quality up, week by week

I keep two routines. At the start and the end of every cycle we set the targets for new enrolments and for re-enrolments, so I know at any point how many students we have, how many are likely to continue, and how many I still need to bring in. The action list from that planning is then reviewed in a standing meeting every Tuesday, which is where anything that has not moved gets picked up.
